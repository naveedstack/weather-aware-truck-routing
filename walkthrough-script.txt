================================================================================
WHAT IT IS                                                             0:25
================================================================================

[ON SCREEN] The app, empty form, map of the US.

"Hi, I'm [your name]. This is my Spotter full stack submission. It's a
weather-aware truck routing app, Django on the back end, React on the front.

So, the problem. Normal navigation tells you which route is fastest. It doesn't
know that a forty-five mile an hour crosswind is a serious problem for a loaded
semi, or when your truck actually gets to that wind. So this takes truck routes,
looks up the hourly forecast along each one at the time the truck arrives there,
and weighs that against the load."


================================================================================
THE DEMO                                                               1:50
================================================================================

[ON SCREEN] Type "Lub" slowly in the From field, pick Lubbock, TX.

"Let me plan a real trip. As I type it's suggesting cities, that's TomTom's
search. They're only suggestions though, I can type a full address or a ZIP and
it'll still plan it."

[ON SCREEN] Type "Den", pick Denver, CO.

"Departure defaults to the next full hour in my own time zone, load to
thirty-five thousand pounds, and down here I pick how often to check the
weather. Every ten, twenty-five or fifty miles. I'll leave it on twenty-five."

[ON SCREEN] Click Plan trip, let the loading state play.

"While that runs it tells me what the server's doing, and those grey blocks keep
the layout from jumping."

[ON SCREEN] Results arrive. STOP TALKING FOR TWO SECONDS.

"Okay. Three routes. The recommended one's dark cyan and drawn on top, the
others are muted. Every dot is a checkpoint, coloured by the risk at the time
the truck gets there. Not the weather right now.

The cards give me distance, travel time, average risk, arrival, and the miles at
each risk level. And this line tells me the one thing that decided it."

[ON SCREEN] Read the actual reason text off the card.

"So it names the runner-up and the margin, and I can disagree with it if I want."

[ON SCREEN] Hover another route card, then click it.

"Hovering a card lifts that route on the map. Clicking picks it."

[ON SCREEN] Hover a checkpoint mid-route, then click it.

"Now the checkpoints. Hovering one gives me the short version. Which checkpoint,
how bad, when the truck's there, and what's driving it. Here it's the wind.

Clicking gives me all of it. The ETA, the forecast hour it landed on, then wind,
gusts, rain and snow, each with its own level. The overall one is the worst of
the three. And if a load rule pushed it up, it says which rule."

[ON SCREEN] Close the popup. Drag the slider slowly to +18h, then to +36h.

"And the heatmap. Each square is the weather risk in a ten-mile band around the
routes, and the slider walks through forty-eight hours.

Watch two things. The cells recolour instantly, because all forty-nine hours
came down with the plan. Nothing's going back to the server. And the truck
markers move. That's where each truck actually is at that hour. So route A's
truck is about here, and that's the weather it's driving into."


================================================================================
THE LOGIC                                            1:40 - take your time
================================================================================

[ON SCREEN] Editor: backend/trips/domain/risk.py

"Right, the logic. Thresholds live here as a table rather than a pile of
if-statements. The spec's table left a couple of edges open. Rain of exactly
nought point two five lands in two bands, thirty-four point six miles an hour
lands in neither. So each threshold carries a flag for whether its boundary
counts, and the rule I picked is that a value in two bands takes the higher one.
Every boundary in that table has a test on it."

[ON SCREEN] Scroll to apply_load_rules.

"Load rules are here. Over thirty thousand pounds, severe wind becomes no
travel. Over forty thousand, high wind becomes severe. They only ever push a
level up, and the comparison's strict, so exactly thirty thousand doesn't
trigger it."

[ON SCREEN] Back to the browser. Change the load to 45,000 and re-plan.

"Let me show you that. Same trip, same departure, load up to forty-five
thousand."

[ON SCREEN] Results arrive. Point at the levels and totals that moved.

"Same weather, worse risk. Only the weight changed."

[ON SCREEN] Editor: backend/trips/planner.py

"One thing on timing, because this is easy to get wrong. The ETAs come from
TomTom's per-point progress data, interpolated between the points. I'm not
taking the distance and dividing by an average speed. If the ETA's off, then
looking up the weather for that time doesn't tell you anything."

[ON SCREEN] Editor: backend/trips/domain/ranking.py

"And the ranking. The spec gave four criteria, starting with fewest severe
miles. I put one in front, fewest no-travel miles. Without it, a route with
no-travel conditions could still win on severe miles alone, and the app ends up
recommending something the driver shouldn't drive. No travel is its own level in
the spec's table, so ranking on it is consistent. The other four are untouched
and in order. It's written up in the PRD."


================================================================================
CODE AND TESTS                                                         1:05
================================================================================

[ON SCREEN] Project tree, expanded to backend/trips and frontend/src.

"Quickly on structure. This domain folder is all the trip logic as plain Python.
No Django, no HTTP, no network calls. Clients wraps TomTom and Open-Meteo. And
the planner runs one plan inside a twenty-second budget, so if the search for
extra routes runs out of time it gives up rather than overrun, and tells the
user why.

On the front end the map is one MapLibre instance. Layers get added once, after
that I only change the data and the feature state. That's why the slider's
smooth, it never rebuilds a layer."

[ON SCREEN] Run the tests. Wait for green, then point at the numbers.

"And the tests. Four hundred and ninety-three of them. The planner and API tests
run the whole pipeline against fake TomTom and Open-Meteo services, with real
route data trimmed down, so it's covered end to end without hitting a live API.
Ruff, TypeScript and the linter are all clean."
